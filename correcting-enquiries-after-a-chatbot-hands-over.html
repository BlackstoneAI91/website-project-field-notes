Correcting Enquiries After a Chatbot Hands Over: A Policy Guide for Malaysian Service Teams

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

A practical guide for Malaysian service business owners and enquiry staff on defining a correction policy when a chatbot hands an enquiry to a service app, separating original messages, machine summaries and staff corrections, with an example correction record and an acceptance exercise.

Why a Correction Policy Matters at the Handover Point

A chatbot that answers first and then hands an enquiry to a service app creates three layers of information that are easy to blur. The customer's original words sit in one place. The chatbot's summary of those words sits in another. The staff member's later correction sits in a third. When nobody defines which layer is authoritative, the team can end up acting on a machine summary that quietly dropped a detail, or overwriting a customer's own words with a paraphrase that seemed clearer at the time.

A correction policy is a short written rule set that says what is preserved, what may be edited, who may edit it, and how a correction is recorded. It is not a technical feature. It is an operating agreement between the people who maintain the enquiry flow and the people who answer the enquiries.

This guide is written for Malaysian service business owners and the staff who maintain customer enquiries. It assumes a chatbot has already been deployed and now hands work to a service app. The aim is to make corrections visible and reversible rather than silent. Separate the Three Records Before You Write Any Rules

Before drafting a policy, list the records that already exist in your enquiry flow. Most handovers produce at least three.

The original message is what the customer typed, said or selected. It should be stored exactly as received, including typos, mixed languages and incomplete sentences. This record is the evidence of what the customer actually asked.

The machine summary is what the chatbot produced from that message: a category, a short description, extracted fields such as service type or preferred date, and perhaps a confidence indicator. This record is a convenience for routing. It is not the customer's statement.

The staff correction is what a person changes after reading the original. It might fix a misread service type, add a location the chatbot missed, or mark an enquiry as out of scope. This record is a human judgement and should carry the name of the person who made it and the time it was made.

A simple test helps: if the machine summary and the original message disagree, which one does your team treat as true? Write the answer down. If the answer is "it depends", the policy is not finished. Keeping the original message immutable and treating the summary as a draft is usually the safer default, because it preserves the customer's own account for later review.

Draft the Correction Rules in Plain Language

A workable policy fits on one page. The following clauses are a starting structure; adapt the wording to your own team and service scope.

First, state that the original message is never edited. Staff may annotate it but not rewrite it.

Second, state that the machine summary is a draft. It may be corrected, but the correction must be recorded as a separate entry rather than typed over the summary.

Third, name who may correct. Typically this is the staff member handling the enquiry, with a second person able to review corrections above a certain impact, such as a change of service category or a change that affects quoted scope.

Fourth, define what a correction record must contain: the enquiry reference, the field changed, the value before, the value after, the reason, the person, and the time.

Fifth, define what happens when the customer later clarifies. A customer's follow-up message is a new original message, not a correction to the old one. Link it to the same enquiry rather than merging the text.

Sixth, define retention. Decide how long original messages, summaries and corrections are kept, and make sure the retention period is consistent with your privacy commitments. Do not copy personal contact details into shared notes; keep them in the system that is designed to hold them.

Seventh, define escalation. If a correction changes the meaning of the enquiry in a way that affects price, eligibility or timing, the policy should say who is told before the customer receives a revised answer.

An Example Correction Record

The following is a hypothetical example for illustration. It does not describe a real customer or a real deployment.

Enquiry reference: ENQ-1042. Channel: website chatbot. Received: 14 March, 10:12.

Original message, preserved unchanged: "Hi, need someone to look at the aircond at my shop in Satok, it is not cold since yesterday, can come this week?"

Machine summary as generated: Service category: general maintenance. Location: not captured. Preferred timing: this week. Confidence: medium.

Staff correction, entry one. Field: service category. Before: general maintenance. After: diagnostic visit. Reason: customer reports a fault, not routine servicing. Corrected by: staff member A. Time: 14 March, 10:31.

Staff correction, entry two. Field: location. Before: blank. After: Satok, as stated in the original message. Reason: chatbot did not extract the area named by the customer. Corrected by: staff member A. Time: 14 March, 10:32.

Review note: because the category changed from maintenance to diagnostic, the policy requires a second reviewer before the visit is scheduled. Reviewer B confirms at 10:45.

Notice what the record does not do. It does not rewrite the customer's sentence. It does not delete the machine's original guess. It shows the sequence, so anyone reading later can see what the chatbot thought, what the person decided, and why. That sequence is what makes the handover auditable.

Run an Acceptance Exercise Before You Rely on the Policy

A policy that has never been tested is a hope. Run a small acceptance exercise with the staff who actually answer enquiries. The exercise below is an author heuristic, not a published standard, and the pass marks are suggestions you should adjust.

Step one: prepare five test enquiries. Include one where the chatbot summary is correct, one where it misreads the service type, one where it misses a location, one written in mixed languages, and one where the customer sends a follow-up that changes the request.

Step two: ask two staff members to process each enquiry using only the written policy. Do not coach them during the exercise.

Step three: check the outputs against four questions. Is the original message unchanged in every case? Is every change recorded as a separate entry with a reason and a name? Did the follow-up get linked rather than merged? Did any correction trigger the escalation rule where it should have?

Step four: score each enquiry as pass or fail on those four questions. A simple target is all four passes on all five enquiries before the policy is treated as live; treat that as an example threshold, not a guarantee of quality.

Step five: record where staff hesitated. Hesitation usually points to a clause that is ambiguous or a field that the service app does not yet capture. Fix the clause or the field, then rerun the exercise with fresh test enquiries.

Repeat the exercise whenever the chatbot's behaviour changes, the service menu changes, or a new staff member joins the enquiry desk.

Keep the Policy Alive as the Chatbot and App Change

Correction policies decay quietly. A chatbot is retrained, a new service category is added, a staff member leaves, and the written rules no longer match what the team does. Three habits slow that decay.

First, review the policy on a fixed date each year, and also whenever the chatbot's prompts, categories or handover triggers change. Put the review date in the document itself.

Second, keep a short log of corrections that were disputed. A disputed correction is the best evidence that a rule is unclear or that a field is missing.

Third, keep the policy close to the people who maintain the enquiry flow. If the chatbot and the service app are maintained by different people, the policy should name both roles and say who owns each clause.

Where the chatbot itself is being revised, the material on AI chatbot development covers the build side; this page covers what happens after the handover. Where the service app is the system of record, the correction log described above belongs in that app, not in a separate spreadsheet that drifts out of date.

None of this requires a large team. It requires that the three records stay separate, that corrections are written down with a reason, and that someone checks the policy still matches reality.

Where This Fits in a Wider Enquiry System

A correction policy is one part of a larger enquiry system. The chatbot handles the first contact, the service app holds the record, and the website shapes what the customer asks in the first place. Clear service pages and well-worded enquiry forms reduce the number of corrections needed, because the customer's first message is more likely to contain the details the team needs.

For businesses in Sarawak, the local context matters. Service areas, travel time and language mix all affect how an enquiry should be read, and a correction policy that ignores those realities will be corrected by staff informally. Blackstone Intelligence works with Malaysian businesses on AI automation, workflow automation, website development and software development, and its public materials describe an approach that connects websites, AI agents and workflows rather than treating them as isolated deliverables. That framing is useful here: the correction policy is the seam between the chatbot and the app, and it deserves the same attention as either system on its own.

Start with the three records, write the rules in plain language, test them with real staff, and review on a fixed date. That is enough to make the handover trustworthy.
