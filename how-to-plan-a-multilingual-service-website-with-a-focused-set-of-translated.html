Planning a Focused Multilingual Service Website

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

A planning worksheet for Malaysian businesses choosing which service pages to translate, how to guide readers between languages and how to keep both versions current.

Start With a Shortlist of Pages

The usual mistake in multilingual planning is to begin with languages. A team decides it wants English, Malay and Chinese, then asks a web team to translate everything. That approach multiplies maintenance work and often produces thin, half-finished pages in every language. A better starting point is a shortlist of pages that earn their place in a second language.

Ask one question per page. Would a customer who reads only that language make a decision from this page? A service overview page usually passes. A news archive from four years ago usually fails. A contact page passes. A staff hobby page fails. The owner of this step should be the business owner, working with whoever manages the website content.

Write the shortlist down before any translation begins. A practical target for a small service business is three to six translated pages per language, covering the home page, one or two core service pages, a contact or enquiry page, and a short about page. That is a deliberate set, not a mirror. It is easier to keep accurate and easier for a visitor to navigate.

Keep the shortlist in a simple table with columns for page name, purpose, primary audience, and whether it exists in the source language. If a page does not exist in the source language, it cannot be translated. Fix the source first.

Decide Which Language Serves Which Customer

Malaysia is a multilingual market, and a service business may serve customers who read English, Bahasa Malaysia, Mandarin or Chinese, or a mix. The planning question is not which languages are popular in general. It is which languages your actual enquiries arrive in.

A useful exercise is to review the last few months of enquiry messages and note the language each customer used. The person who handles enquiries, often an admin or front desk lead, is best placed to do this. Count the languages, not the messages. If a language appears rarely, a translated page may not be worth the upkeep yet.

Be careful not to overstate what this tells you. A small sample of enquiries is a signal, not proof of market demand. Treat it as one input alongside what customers ask for directly and what your team can realistically maintain.

For each language you keep, write one sentence describing the reader. For example, a reader who prefers Mandarin and is comparing service providers in Kuching. That sentence guides tone, examples and even which service is listed first. A translation that ignores the reader becomes a word-for-word copy that feels foreign.

Record the decision and the reason. When someone asks in a year why a language was dropped, the note answers the question.

Build a Page Selection Worksheet

A worksheet keeps the plan honest. Fill it in before briefing a translator or a web team. The columns below are a suggested format, not a standard.

Column one, page name. Column two, source language version ready, answered yes or no. Column three, target language. Column four, decision, which is translate, adapt or skip. Column five, named owner. Column six, review date.

The difference between translate and adapt matters. Translate means the content carries over closely. Adapt means examples, currency references, place names or service descriptions change for the reader. A service page aimed at customers in Kuching may mention travel or site visits differently from a page aimed at Kuala Lumpur readers. Mark those pages adapt so the translator knows the job is not mechanical.

Here is a hypothetical example. A small air-conditioning servicing business in Sarawak, referred to only by role, decides to add a Mandarin version. Its owner lists the home page, the servicing page and the contact page as translate. The about page is marked skip because it is mostly staff photos and a short history. The owner assigns the servicing page to the operations lead for a factual check and sets a review date three months out.

Keep the worksheet where the team can see it. A shared document is enough. The point is that every translated page has a named owner and a reason.

Plan Navigation and Language Switching

A focused set of translated pages needs a clear way to move between languages. Visitors should be able to tell which language they are reading and switch without losing their place.

Decide the switching behaviour early. Two common patterns are a language menu in the header and a short line in the footer. Either works if it is consistent. The website project lead should confirm that switching from a translated service page lands on the matching page in the other language where one exists, and shows a clearly labelled alternative when no translation exists. A switch that always dumps the visitor on the home page is frustrating.

Label languages in the language itself where possible, so a reader recognises their own. Keep the labels short and place them in the same spot on every page.

Navigation labels also need translation, not just body text. A menu that reads Services in English and something inconsistent in another language looks unfinished. Give the translator the menu labels as a separate short list so they are handled deliberately.

Finally, decide what happens to pages that are not translated. They can stay in the source language with a small note, or be hidden from the translated menu. Hiding them keeps the translated site tidy but may confuse a visitor who saw the page mentioned elsewhere. Choose one approach and write it down.

Set Content Rules Before Translation Starts

Translation quality depends heavily on what the translator receives. Give clear rules up front and the work goes faster.

First, freeze the source text. Editing the English page while it is being translated creates mismatched versions. The content owner should approve the source page before it goes out.

Second, supply a short glossary of business terms. Service names, product names and any technical phrases should be translated the same way every time. Include the business name and decide whether it stays in its original form.

Third, state the tone. A friendly, plain style reads differently from a formal one. Give one or two example sentences in the target language that show the tone you want.

Fourth, decide how to handle forms and confirmation messages. A translated contact page with an English confirmation message breaks the experience. List every message a visitor might see and translate those too.

Fifth, keep claims accurate. If a service page states what the business does, the translated version must say the same thing, not a stronger version. Reviewers should compare meaning, not just wording.

A technology consultancy handling website development can support this stage by setting up a simple review workflow, but the business owner remains responsible for factual accuracy in every language.

Run Acceptance Checks and Plan Upkeep

Before a translated page goes live, run a short acceptance check. These checks are practical habits, not a formal standard.

Check that the page loads and reads well on a phone. Check that the language switch works from that page. Check that every link points to a page that exists in the chosen language, or to a clearly marked fallback. Check that the enquiry form sends and that the confirmation message appears in the right language. Check that names, addresses and contact details match the source exactly.

Have one person who reads the target language review the page for meaning, not just grammar. The reviewer should be named in the worksheet. A second reader is useful for the home page and any page that carries commercial claims.

Plan upkeep at the same time. Translated pages drift out of date when only the source language is updated. Add a review date to each row of the worksheet and assign the check to a named role, such as the marketing coordinator. A quarterly review is a reasonable starting rhythm for a small business, though the right interval depends on how often your services change.
